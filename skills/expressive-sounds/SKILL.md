# Expressive Sounds

<!-- expressive-sounds-runtime:start -->
Use a sound when it changes the pace, reveals a reaction, or makes an action perceptible. A quiet scene can use none. In an ordinary scene, a few well-placed sounds are enough. Increase density around beats that earn it, such as suspense, exertion, comedy, adult intimacy, or physical action. In an adult erotic scene or a fight/action sequence with sparse dialogue, include several written sound forms across meaningful beats: use character vocalizations and, where supported by the action, non-vocal sounds from touch, movement, clothing, impacts, or nearby objects. Breathing described only as narration is not a substitute for written sound forms. Keep them varied and moderate; do not stack them, repeat them mechanically, or make the scene read like a comic book. Omit them only if the brief explicitly requires silence or forbids audible reactions. Leave room for speech and silence.

| Sound or reaction | Useful forms | When it earns space |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thought or hesitation | `Mm.`, `Hmm...`, `Um...` | A character needs a moment before deciding or replying. |
| Surprise or recognition | `Oh!`, `Ah.`, `Huh?` | A discovery changes the character's understanding. |
| Relief or appreciation | `Ah...`, `Mm.`, a sigh | Tension releases or a character notices something pleasant. |
| Fear or tension | A caught breath, a small gasp | The interruption matters. Describe a swallow rather than writing `Gulp!` in realistic prose. |
| Effort or impact | `Oof.`, a short grunt | Weight, movement, or physical effort interrupts speech. |
| Laughter | `Ha.`, a quiet laugh | Distinguish delight, nerves, disbelief, or irony through the surrounding beat. |
| Attention or quiet | `Psst.`, `Shh.` | A character actively redirects another person's attention or asks for silence. |
| Environment | `click`, `tap`, `tick`, `rustle`, `creak`, `thud`, `splash` | An audible action matters. Prefer a natural sentence such as “The latch clicks shut.” |
| Intoxication, when present | `Hic.`, a swallowed `Hh—`, a wet laugh; glass `clinks`, a stool `scrapes`, a drink `sloshes` | Use only a sound supported by the moment. A hiccup is not a required drunk-character tic; ordinary glass and furniture sounds often feel more natural. |

These are examples, not a closed vocabulary. Use ordinary English spellings and vary reactions by character. A reserved character may barely react. An expansive character may laugh or exclaim more readily. A repeated pattern should express character or track a changing event. Preserve proper names, but keep spoken words and common sound expressions in English.

**Duration and repetition.** Use short forms by default. A small extension such as `Mmm...` can suggest a sustained sound. Two vocalizations can mark separate reactions, especially if a pause or gesture changes the meaning of the second. A repeated sound such as `Tap, tap.` means two contacts. A stretched spelling represents one sustained sound. Prefer a pause, action, or change in timing to extreme spelling or stacked punctuation. Reserve capitals and strongly stylized effects for a brief that calls for them.

**Rhythm and genre.** A period contains a reaction. An ellipsis can let it trail off, a question mark adds uncertainty, and an exclamation mark adds force. Build suspense by changing one feature at a time, such as the interval between knocks. Keep realistic scenes restrained, give comedy more room for exaggeration, and use short sounds in action where they clarify movement. In visual novel dialogue, put each spoken turn on its own speaker-labelled line and any intervening action in a separate paragraph.

**Character and situation.** Choose vocalizations, pauses, and environmental sounds that fit the speaker and the immediate beat. A reserved person may answer with a small `Mm.`; an already-intoxicated character might hiccup once, swallow a word, laugh at the wrong moment, or knock a glass against the bar, but do not add those behaviors automatically. Keep speech readable and individualized. Do not write a whole accent phonetically, make drunkenness a string of `hic`/`glug` noises, or use the same interjection for every character. A spoken or written sound should reveal a reaction, affect rhythm, or make an action perceptible.

**Adult intimacy.** Use several context-appropriate sound forms over the scene, such as `Mm...`, `Ah...`, a brief laugh, `rustle`, `tap`, or a soft `thump`, when the action supports them. Let sounds become somewhat more noticeable as the scene intensifies, while varying their form and leaving space for dialogue, gestures, and quiet. Include both vocal and non-vocal sounds when each fits; a description of breathing by itself is insufficient. Preserve the characters' individual voices and any explicit consent required by the brief. A vocalization alone does not establish consent. Write the sounds as natural English prose or dialogue, not as comic-book effect labels unless the requested style calls for that.

**Spoken output.** For voice actors or speech synthesis, prefer pronounceable spellings and readable pauses. Repeated letters do not guarantee duration. Check the actual voice if timing matters. Written sound words and vocalizations belong to prose or dialogue. An SFX cue is a separate production instruction and requires authorization under the scene format.

On review, keep each sound that adds information or rhythm. Trim interchangeable reactions, repeated effects with no new beat, and spellings that are hard to read aloud.
